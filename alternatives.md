# Alternatives
This section explores how each decision-maker might make the decision today without our proposed tool. The purpose is not to claim that CPSC or NFHS follows this exact process, but to describe a plausible workflow based on publicly available information.

### Audience A — CPSC Epidemiology
Role and Decision

Role: Associate Executive Director for Epidemiology at the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)

Decision: Determine whether severe treadmill injury mechanisms among adults age 65 and older justify deeper investigation or possible standards work.

What might trigger the decision?

The process might begin when an injury pattern appears unusual in NEISS data or another source of consumer-product injury information.

Possible triggers:

- An increase in treadmill-related emergency-department visits among adults age 65 and older.
- A high proportion of severe outcomes, such as hospitalization.
- Repeated injury narratives describing similar mechanisms.
- Consumer complaints or incident reports involving treadmills.
- Reports of serious injuries or deaths.
- Questions raised by CPSC staff, researchers, manufacturers, or standards organizations.

NEISS could be useful as an early screening signal because it can show national estimates and patterns across age groups, diagnoses, body parts, and product categories.

What information might be used?

A CPSC epidemiology decision-maker might examine:

- NEISS national injury estimates.
- Patient age.
- Diagnosis.
- Body part injured.
- Disposition, especially hospitalization.
- Product codes.
- Injury narratives.
- Trends across multiple years.
- Differences between adults age 65–74, 75–84, and 85+.

Additional information might include:

- CPSC incident reports.
- Death certificates or fatality investigations.
- Consumer complaints.
- Existing treadmill safety standards.
- Previous recalls or investigations.
- Engineering information about treadmill design.
- Research about balance, mobility, and fall risk among older adults.
- Manufacturer or industry information.
- Who might be involved?

Possible participants include:

- Epidemiologists and data analysts.
- Engineers.
- Human factors specialists.
- Health scientists.
- Economists.
- Legal or regulatory staff.
- Voluntary standards staff.
- Product safety investigators.
- Manufacturers or industry representatives.
- Standards-development organizations.

Epidemiologists might identify the pattern, while engineers and human-factors specialists could evaluate whether a particular treadmill design or user interaction could plausibly contribute to the injury mechanism.

### Possible decision process
1）Identify a signal in NEISS.
- Analysts notice that severe treadmill injuries among adults age 65+ appear frequent or concentrated around a specific injury mechanism.
2）Examine the pattern more closely.
- Analysts compare age groups, diagnoses, body parts, hospitalizations, and narratives.
3）Review individual injury narratives.
Staff look for repeated mechanisms, such as:
- Falling while getting onto or off the treadmill.
- Losing balance while the belt is moving.
- Being propelled backward.
- Striking nearby furniture or walls.
4）Compare with other information sources.
CPSC staff might examine complaints, incident reports, fatalities, previous recalls, and engineering information.
5）Evaluate whether the mechanism appears preventable.
Engineers and human-factors specialists might consider whether a treadmill feature, warning, control, or design change could reduce the risk.
6）Review existing standards.
Staff might determine whether current voluntary standards already address the mechanism.
7）Decide whether additional work is justified.
Possible outcomes could include:
- No additional action.
- More detailed data analysis.
- A focused investigation.
- Additional research.
- Discussion with a voluntary standards organization.
- Development of a recommendation for future standards or regulatory work.

## Where might the process be difficult or uncertain?

The biggest challenge is that NEISS shows injuries associated with products, but it does not automatically show that the product caused the injury.

Important uncertainties include:

- NEISS does not provide treadmill exposure hours.
- Older adults may use treadmills differently from younger adults.
- Some injuries may result from underlying health or balance conditions.
- Narratives may not contain enough detail to identify the exact mechanism.
- Product design information may be missing.
- The same injury could result from user behavior, environment, or product design.
- A high number of injuries does not necessarily mean that a design change would prevent them.

Therefore, NEISS would likely be most useful as a screening signal that identifies where deeper investigation may be needed, rather than as proof that a treadmill feature should be regulated.

### Audience B — NFHS Soccer
Role and Decision

Role: Director of Sports and Soccer Rules Committee liaison at the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS)

Decision: Determine which adolescent soccer injury mechanism should become a Point of Emphasis or enter a prospective pilot study.

What might trigger the decision?

The process could begin when data or reports suggest that one soccer injury mechanism is especially common, severe, or preventable.

Possible triggers:

- A recurring pattern of adolescent soccer injuries in NEISS.
- High rates of concussion or head injury.
- Player-to-player collisions.
- Goalkeeper collisions.
- Injuries related to heading.
- Lower-extremity injuries associated with particular types of contact or play.
- Concerns raised by coaches, officials, athletic trainers, state associations, or medical experts.
- Evidence that an existing rule is inconsistently understood or enforced.

NEISS could provide an early signal, but NFHS would likely need high-school-specific evidence before making a rule-related decision.

What information might be used?

A decision-maker might consider:

- NEISS adolescent soccer injury patterns.
- Injury narratives.
- Diagnosis and body part.
- Hospitalization or severity.
- Patient age.
- High School RIO or similar high-school injury surveillance data.
- Athlete exposure data.
- Whether the injury occurred in practice or competition.
- Game situations associated with the injury.
- Existing NFHS soccer rules.
- Current Points of Emphasis.
- Research on concussion and sports injury prevention.
- Feedback from state associations.

High-school-specific surveillance could be especially important because NEISS includes emergency-department visits but does not necessarily indicate whether the injury occurred in an NFHS-sanctioned game.

Who might be involved?

Possible participants include:

- NFHS Director of Sports.
- Soccer Rules Committee members.
- NFHS Sports Medicine Advisory Committee.
- State high school association representatives.
- Coaches.
- Referees and other officials.
- Athletic trainers.
- Sports medicine researchers.
- School administrators.
- Possibly student-athlete representatives.

Different groups could contribute different perspectives:

- Athletic trainers may understand injury severity and recurrence.
- Officials may know whether dangerous behavior is already prohibited.
- Coaches may identify unintended consequences of a proposed rule.
- Researchers may evaluate whether an intervention is likely to reduce injury risk.
- State associations may help determine whether a pilot is practical.
## Possible decision process
1）Identify a potential injury mechanism.
NEISS or another dataset shows that one mechanism appears important among adolescent soccer players.
2）Check whether high-school-specific data support the pattern.
NFHS could compare the NEISS signal with High School RIO or other injury-surveillance systems.
3）Define the mechanism precisely.
 Possible examples include:
- Player-to-player head contact.
- Goalkeeper-player collisions.
- Heading-related concussion.
- Dangerous challenges.
- Falls after contact.
4）Review the current rules.
The Soccer Rules Committee could determine whether the behavior is already covered by an existing rule.
5）Gather expert and stakeholder input.
Coaches, officials, athletic trainers, medical experts, and state associations could explain how the mechanism occurs in actual games.

6）Choose an intervention.
If the behavior is already illegal but enforcement or awareness is inconsistent, NFHS might consider a Point of Emphasis.

If the proposed intervention is new or could significantly change gameplay, NFHS might prefer a prospective pilot study.

7） Measure results.
A pilot could track:
- Frequency of the target injury.
- Severity.
- Athlete exposures.
- Penalties.
- Referee behavior.
- Unintended consequences.
8）Decide whether broader action is justified.
Possible outcomes include:
- No rule change.
- Additional education.
- Point of Emphasis.
- Additional data collection.
- Pilot study.
- Formal rules proposal.

## Where might the process be difficult or uncertain?

Important limitations include:

- NEISS includes emergency-department injuries, but NFHS needs evidence specifically relevant to organized high-school soccer.
- NEISS does not provide athlete exposure.
- A rule change could alter player behavior in unexpected ways.
- Officials may interpret a new rule differently.
- Coaches and players may adjust tactics.
- Injury reductions may be difficult to separate from changes in participation, training, or reporting.

Therefore, NEISS would probably be most useful for identifying which injury mechanism deserves closer investigation, while high-school-specific surveillance and prospective testing could help determine what NFHS should actually do.

## Evidence and Caution

Publicly available information supports several parts of this role-play.

For CPSC:

- NEISS is used to identify patterns in consumer-product injuries.
- CPSC participates in voluntary standards activities.
- Injury data, incident information, research, and technical analysis can contribute to product-safety work.

For NFHS:

- High-school injury surveillance data are used to study sports injury patterns.
- NFHS publishes sport-specific Points of Emphasis.
- Sports rules committees participate in recommending rule changes.

However, public sources do not establish that either organization follows the exact step-by-step process described above.

For that reason, this role-play uses language such as:

- "might"
- "could"
- "may consider"
- "a plausible process"

rather than claiming that CPSC or NFHS definitely follows this exact workflow.
