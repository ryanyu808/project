# Objectives

## Proposed audiences and decisions

**Audience A - CPSC Epidemiology.** An Associate Executive Director for Epidemiology at the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) must decide whether severe treadmill injury mechanisms among adults age 65 and older justify deeper investigation and standards work.

**Audience B - NFHS Soccer.** The Director of Sports and a Soccer Rules Committee liaison at the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS) must decide which adolescent soccer injury mechanism should enter a Point of Emphasis or a prospective pilot study.


## Audience A - CPSC Epidemiology

### Sourced facts

- NEISS can estimate national emergency-department injury burden using survey weights.
- Narratives may help identify possible injury mechanisms, but they require validation because the text is short and inconsistent.
- `Disposition` provides a limited measure of acute severity.
- NEISS can help identify hazards worth investigating, but it cannot by itself establish that a treadmill design or feature caused an injury.

### AI-generated hypotheses

#### Desired outcome

The decision maker would want to know whether putting resources, such as funding, staff time, and opportunity cost, into a deeper investigation is worthwhile. The key is not only whether the injury burden is large, but whether the issue is severe and consistent enough, with a plausible path toward safety action, that further investigation could identify a hazard worth addressing and potentially reduce serious injuries.

#### Risks if the decision is wrong

A wrong decision could lead CPSC to investigate a weak or misleading hazard signal, which would waste limited staff time and delay attention to more important safety issues. On the other hand, deciding not to investigate a real hazard could allow a meaningful safety problem to remain unidentified or unaddressed.

#### Tradeoffs and constraints

The decision maker has to balance the depth of one investigation against the ability to address other safety issues. Spending more time and resources on one treadmill mechanism may produce stronger evidence, but it also reduces the resources available for other potential hazards.

There is also a tradeoff between acting early on an uncertain signal and waiting for stronger evidence before committing more resources.

#### Evidence that could change the decision

I would be more willing to support a deeper investigation if the NEISS data show a consistent injury mechanism associated with a meaningful weighted burden of severe injuries, especially if the pattern remains after narrative validation.

Evidence from similar investigations, past CPSC experience, or comparable safety research could further strengthen the case that additional investigation is likely to produce useful results.

#### Measures of success

I would consider the investigation successful if it produces credible, data-supported, and actionable insight about a treadmill safety issue and provides a clear basis for deciding whether further safety action or standards work is justified.

In the longer term, the ultimate success would be whether the resulting intervention contributes to reducing severe treadmill injuries among adults age 65 and older.

### Questions for a real stakeholder

- How large or severe does an injury signal need to be before CPSC decides that a focused investigation is worth the resources?
- How does CPSC balance the risk of investigating a weak signal against the risk of overlooking a real hazard?
- What additional evidence is normally required before a NEISS pattern can influence standards work?
- How does CPSC measure whether a hazard investigation was successful?


## Audience B - NFHS Soccer

### Sourced facts

- NEISS can identify soccer-related emergency-department visits and describe injury type, body part, disposition, age, sex, and narrative information.
- Narratives may provide clues about mechanisms such as contact, falls, twisting, ball impact, goal contact, or field-related incidents.
- NEISS does not identify whether an injury occurred during an NFHS-sanctioned game or practice.
- NEISS lacks participation and athlete-exposure data and cannot establish that a particular rule caused or prevented an injury.

### AI-generated hypotheses

#### Desired outcome

Through analyzing injury patterns, the decision maker would want to identify which injury mechanisms or conditions occur frequently among adolescent soccer injuries and may be relevant to current rules.

The goal is to prioritize mechanisms that are both important and realistically actionable through rules, education, or enforcement. Some injury risks are inherent to participating in soccer and may be difficult to eliminate without fundamentally changing the sport.

Additional evidence or a pilot study could then be used to evaluate whether a proposed change is feasible and likely to improve player safety.

#### Risks if the decision is wrong

A wrong decision could result from attributing injuries to the wrong factor or mechanism. This could lead to changing a rule that may have been providing a protective effect, creating unintended consequences and potentially leading to more or more severe injuries.

Even if injuries do not increase, an unnecessary rule change could disrupt gameplay or create additional enforcement and compliance burden without producing meaningful safety benefits.

#### Tradeoffs and constraints

The decision maker needs to balance player safety with the playability and integrity of the sport. Some measures may reduce injuries, but they could also interrupt the flow of the game or change how the sport is played.

If a rule becomes too restrictive, it may reduce participation or interest in the sport even if it provides some safety benefit.

#### Evidence that could change the decision

I would be more willing to support a rule-study pilot if additional participation or athlete-exposure data show that the injury mechanism remains important after accounting for how often athletes are exposed to it, and if the proposed intervention appears realistically enforceable.

Evidence from similar sports organizations, other countries, or previous rule changes could also help show whether a similar approach has been feasible and effective.

#### Measures of success

I would consider the decision successful if the targeted injuries, especially severe injuries, decrease, while the flow and overall experience of the game are not significantly affected.

Participation and interest in the sport should also remain relatively stable compared with before the change.

### Questions for a real stakeholder

- What makes an injury issue important enough to become an NFHS Point of Emphasis or rule-study pilot?
- How does NFHS decide whether an injury mechanism is realistically actionable through rules, education, or enforcement?
- How much safety improvement would justify a rule that affects the flow or experience of the game?
- What evidence would NFHS need before moving from a pilot study to a permanent rule change?


## Comparison of the two audiences

Both audiences are trying to improve safety, but the decisions they face are different.

For CPSC, the main question is whether an injury signal is strong enough to justify spending limited investigation resources. The decision therefore centers on whether the expected safety value of a deeper investigation outweighs the opportunity cost and uncertainty involved.

For NFHS, identifying an important injury mechanism is not enough. The issue must also be actionable through a rule, education effort, or enforcement change. NFHS therefore has to balance potential safety benefits against effects on gameplay, enforcement, participation, and interest in the sport.

The CPSC decision focuses more on whether a hazard deserves deeper investigation, while the NFHS decision focuses more on whether an identified safety issue can be addressed in a practical way without creating greater unintended costs.
